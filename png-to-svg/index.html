The short version

Tracing (vectorizing) looks at your PNG and rebuilds it out of paths — outlined shapes filled with solid colour — instead of a grid of pixels. The payoff is that the result is resolution-independent: the same SVG is razor-sharp on a business card and on a billboard, and it's usually tiny when the source is a simple graphic.

That only works when the picture is made of shapes to begin with: logos, icons, symbols, line art, a screenshot of an interface. Hand it a photograph and there are no clean shapes to find — just millions of gradients — so the trace comes out blurry, posterised and often larger than the PNG you started with. If that's your case, don't; keep the PNG or use JPG.

What traces beautifully

  • Logos and wordmarks. A flat logo exported as PNG becomes a crisp, scalable SVG you can drop into print, a favicon, or a huge banner.
  • Icons and symbols. Simple, few-colour shapes are exactly what tracing is built for — small files, clean edges.
  • Line art and diagrams. Sharp outlines survive the trip; you get editable vector paths back.
  • UI screenshots of graphics. Flat interface elements with solid fills trace well.

The honest caveats

  • Photos don't vectorize. This is the big one. A photograph has no clean shapes to trace, so the SVG is blurry, posterised and often heavier than the PNG. The converter flags it when your input looks like a photo — believe the flag. For photos, JPG or WebP is the honest answer.
  • Tracing is approximate. Even on flat art it's a re-drawing, not a perfect copy — very fine detail and soft, feathered edges get simplified. For a clean logo that's exactly what you want; for something intricate, check the result.
  • Detail vs size is a real trade. The quality slider controls how many colours the trace follows. Higher follows more detail and makes a bigger file; lower posterises to fewer colours and a smaller one. Nudge it to taste.
  • Nothing uploads, and you can check it. The tracer is pure JavaScript on your machine. Press F12, open Network, trace a PNG: no request. Or cut your Wi-Fi and trace anyway — it still works.

Questions people actually ask

Is this a real vector, or a PNG hidden in an SVG?
A real vector. The output is genuine <path> shapes, which is why it scales without blurring. It is not the PNG wrapped in an <image> tag.
My logo has a transparent background — does that survive?
Yes. Transparent regions are traced as their own areas rather than filled, so the SVG stays usable on any background. Hard edges trace more cleanly than soft, feathered ones.
Why did my traced photo come out huge and blurry?
Because it's a photo. Tracing needs flat shapes; a photograph is continuous gradients, so the tracer approximates it with thousands of paths — big file, soft result. Keep the PNG, or convert it to JPG instead.
Can I edit the SVG afterwards?
Yes — it's standard SVG, so it opens in Illustrator, Inkscape, Figma or a text editor, with the traced paths editable like any vector.
Is PNG to SVG free, with no limit?
Yes — no account, no daily cap, no file limit. It runs on your device, so the only ceiling is your machine.
