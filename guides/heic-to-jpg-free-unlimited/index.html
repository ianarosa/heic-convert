Free, unlimited,
and here's why that's possible.

You hit a daily limit on a free converter and assumed you were being squeezed toward a paid plan. Mostly you weren't. The cap exists because someone is paying for a server, and it goes away when nobody is. That's the whole trick, and it's worth understanding before you trust anyone's "unlimited".

Guide Last checked: July 2026 Reading time: 5 min

The cap isn't greed. It's arithmetic.

When you drop a photo on a typical online converter, the file is uploaded to a machine that company rents. That machine decodes the HEVC, re-encodes a JPG, stores the result until you fetch it, and sends it back. Every one of those steps costs money, per file, forever. Your batch of 400 photos is a line item on somebody's invoice.

Given that, "unlimited and free" would be a promise to let strangers spend unbounded amounts of their money. Nobody sane offers it. So they cap the free tier, sell a paid one to the people who need more, and the free tier exists as a sample. That's a coherent business, honestly run, and the caps aren't a trap — they're the price of the architecture.

The complaint isn't that the cap is unfair. It's that a cap of twenty is no use at all when you have a camera roll of eight hundred — and no amount of goodwill changes that, because the constraint is physics and accounting, not attitude.

Uploading is a legitimate way to build a converter; it's how most of them work. It just means someone else's computer touches your photo, and someone else's bill grows with your batch. Both of those follow from the same decision.

No cap, no account, no watermark

The conversion runs on your machine, so there's no invoice to defend and nothing to meter. Drop in eight hundred photos if you have eight hundred photos.

Why a local converter can honestly say "no limit"

Your browser can decode and re-encode images itself. The standard open-source HEIC decoder, libheif, compiles to WebAssembly — real compiled code running at near-native speed inside a tab. So the page downloads the decoder once, about 1.4 MB, and after that does every conversion on your own CPU.

Which means the marginal cost of your five-hundredth photo, to whoever wrote the page, is zero. Not "low". Zero. There's no server in the loop to bill anyone. Metering it would be inventing a limit for the pleasure of enforcing it, so there's no cap, no account, and no watermark — not out of generosity, but because there's nothing there to charge for.

The privacy consequence is a side effect rather than the point: photos that never go anywhere can't be stored, mined, or covered by a terms-of-service clause. And you can verify the whole claim in ten seconds by turning off your Wi-Fi and converting anyway — that test is on its own page, and it works on any converter, including this one.

So where are the limits here?

"Unlimited" with no asterisks would be its own kind of marketing. There are four real constraints, and none of them is a plan you can upgrade:

  • Your device's memory. Hundreds of photos at once on a desktop is unremarkable. A phone with 3 GB of RAM is still a phone with 3 GB of RAM, and iOS is quick to reclaim memory from a tab, so work in smaller groups there. This is usually far more generous than a free tier — but it is a ceiling, and it's yours.
  • Big zips. Building a zip means holding the archive and the source files in memory at the same time, and browsers cap how much a single blob can hold. Very large batches are more reliable saved individually; the app warns you before you hit the wall rather than after.
  • EXIF is dropped. Dates, camera model, GPS. The photo is decoded to pixels and re-encoded, and the metadata doesn't survive the trip. Rotation is applied, so nothing lands sideways. If your archive needs its timestamps, this isn't the tool for that job yet.
  • Your files get bigger. About 168% of the HEIC at the default quality of 0.85 — 2 MB in, roughly 3.4 MB out. WebP saves about 8% and is still larger than the HEIC; PNG is lossless and roughly twelve times the size. Every HEIC-to-JPG converter does this, uploading or not: it's the codecs, not the tool. Drop the quality toward 0.6 to land near the original size, with loss you'll be able to see.

Two more worth stating: the decoder is a 1.4 MB download on first use, once — a network request that doesn't contain your photo. And the output formats are JPG, PNG, WebP and PDF (see the HEIC to PDF guide). Video and other exotic formats aren't here — if you need those you need a cloud tool, and we'd rather say that than waste your afternoon.

How this is paid for

By ads. There are ad slots on this page and on the converter, marked "Advertisement", and that's the entire revenue model. It's worth saying plainly, because "free and unlimited" ought to prompt the question — and the honest answer to "what's the catch" is more convincing than a claim that there isn't one.

What there isn't: no account to create, no email to hand over, no upsell to a Pro tier, no watermark, and no data to sell, because your files never reach us in the first place. That last one isn't a virtue we're claiming so much as a shape the architecture forced. There's genuinely nothing here to monetise except the space around the tool.

Questions people actually ask

Why do free HEIC converters have daily limits?
Because every file costs them bandwidth, CPU and storage on a server they rent, so their bill scales with your batch. Capping the free tier is the rational design of that business, not greed. A converter running in your browser has no per-file cost to anyone, so it doesn't need a cap.
Is this really unlimited, with no signup?
No daily cap, no account, no watermark, no file count. The genuine limit is your device's memory — hundreds of files at once is normal, and very large batches save more reliably one at a time than as a single zip.
What's the catch?
Ads, marked as ads. Plus the four honest limits above: device memory, big-zip memory, EXIF being dropped, and JPGs coming out around 168% the size of your HEICs.
Is there a watermark on the converted files?
No. The conversion happens in your browser with an open-source decoder; there's no step at which anything could be stamped onto your photo, and no free tier to differentiate from a paid one.
Do I need to install anything or make an account?
Neither. No install, no admin rights, no email. That matters most on a work laptop where you can't install software and wouldn't want to sign up for anything with a work address anyway.
Can I convert to PDF as well?
Yes — JPG, PNG, WebP and PDF, with the same no-upload, no-cap terms. You can make one PDF per photo or combine a batch into a single PDF; the HEIC to PDF guide walks through it.
