Why you'd want a photo as a PDF at all

A PDF isn't a better picture than a JPG — it's the same picture in a different envelope, and the envelope is the point. A PDF opens to the same view on every device and prints predictably, which is exactly what you need when a photo has to behave like a document rather than an image. The usual reasons people end up here:

  • Submitting something. A landlord, a government form, a school portal or an expense tool asks you to "upload a PDF" — and what you have is a photo of a signed page, a receipt or an ID.
  • Printing. A PDF prints at a known page size without a photo printer's cropping and re-scaling surprises.
  • Archiving. One PDF is tidier to file and email than a folder of loose image files, and it stays readable for decades.
  • Sending a set as one thing. Six photos of a contract are far easier for the recipient as a single six-page PDF than as six attachments.

One PDF per photo, or all of them in one

There are two things people mean by "HEIC to PDF", and this does both. Pick whichever matches the job:

  1. Add your photos

    Drag your HEICs onto the page, paste them, or choose a whole folder. There's no file limit and no daily cap — a set of a few hundred is fine. If you're pulling them off an iPhone first, our batch guide covers getting the real HEIC files across rather than auto-converted copies.

  2. Choose PDF, then one file or one each

    Select PDF as the output format. Then decide:

    One combined PDF puts every photo on its own page, in the order you added them, and gives you a single file back. This is what a form or a printer usually wants — reorder the photos before you convert and the pages follow.

    One PDF per photo gives you a separate file for each, which is what you want when each photo is its own document — one receipt, one PDF.

  3. Save

    A combined PDF downloads as one file. One-per-photo PDFs come down individually, or as a single zip for a big batch. Turn your connection back on whenever you like — the work already happened on your machine.

The same no-upload promise, and the same way to check it

Turning a photo into a PDF is the kind of job people most often have a reason to keep private — it's usually a document, an ID, a receipt or a signed page, not a holiday snap. So it matters that this works the way the rest of the site does: the PDF is built inside your browser, and your photo is never sent anywhere.

You don't have to take that on trust. Press F12, open the Network tab, and make a PDF — nothing goes out. Or turn off your Wi-Fi and do it anyway; it works exactly the same, because after the page has loaded there's nothing left for it to talk to. Most "photo to PDF" websites upload your files to a server to build the PDF there. This one has no server to upload to. The without-uploading guide walks through both checks in detail.

The honest caveats

The parts nobody mentions, because a page that only listed upsides would be doing the thing this site exists to avoid:

  • A photo PDF is roughly the size of the JPG inside it. A PDF page wraps a JPG-quality image plus a little document structure, so expect a file close to what the same photo would be as a JPG — which is itself larger than the HEIC you started with. The quality slider still works: lower it for smaller PDFs.
  • EXIF metadata is dropped. Date, camera and GPS don't survive the trip into the PDF. Rotation is applied, so pages come out the right way up. If you need the timestamp and location preserved, this isn't the tool yet.
  • This makes image PDFs, not searchable text. A photo of a page becomes a picture of that page in a PDF — you can't select or search the words in it. That needs OCR, which is a different job and something a cloud service does better than a browser.
  • Very large combined PDFs are limited by your device's memory, not by a plan. That's usually far more generous than a free tier's cap, but a hundred high-resolution photos in one file is real work for a phone.
  • You download a decoder once. About 1.4 MB, the first time you convert anything. That's a network request — just not one carrying your photo.

Questions people actually ask

Can I combine all my photos into a single PDF?
Yes. Choose the combined option and every photo becomes a page in one PDF, in the order you added them. Or choose one-per-photo for a separate file each.
Is the PDF much bigger than the photo?
Not much. It's close to the JPG's size plus a small amount of PDF structure. Drag the quality slider down if you need a smaller file to email or upload.
Does it upload my documents?
No. The PDF is built in your browser and your files never reach a server — which is the whole reason to use this for a document rather than a random upload site. Check it in the Network tab, or convert with Wi-Fi off.
Can I make the PDF's text selectable or searchable?
No. This produces an image PDF — a picture of your photo on a page. Selectable text needs OCR, which this tool doesn't do; a cloud OCR service is the right tool for that.
Does it work on iPhone, iPad, Android and Windows?
Yes, in any recent browser. On iPhone and iPad it's especially fast, because Safari decodes HEIC natively — so you can turn photos into a PDF on the phone that took them, with no app to install.
